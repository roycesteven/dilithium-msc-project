# Research Writing — Instructions for Claude Code

> Source: COMP66060 Workshop, *Research Writing*, Prof. Chenghua Lin, University of Manchester.
> Save as `RESEARCH_WRITING.md` or append to `CLAUDE.md`. Apply every rule below when drafting, editing, or reviewing dissertation text.

## Purpose

These are the rules for writing and assessing the MSc dissertation. When helping draft or edit any section, follow them exactly. The over-arching principle:

**Clarity is King.** If the marker cannot understand what was done, they cannot award marks for it. Write to be understood, not to impress.

## Project constraints (the dissertation)

- **Length:** approx. **8,000 words**.
- **Submission:** a **single PDF file**, due **4 September**.
- Must follow the formatting guidelines and include **all required pages**.
- Assessed against the **`MSc_Report_and_Video_Rubric.pdf`** rubric — consult it; a major aim of the writing is to satisfy the rubric.


## What good technical writing is (and is not)

| Good technical writing is | Good technical writing is **not** |
|---|---|
| Efficient communication of ideas | Ambiguous |
| As clear and simple as possible | Flowery |
| Precise | Filled with unnecessary jargon |
| Interesting and engaging | Boring |

The six main points, applied to every piece of text:

1. Be clear about the **aim** of the writing.
2. Be clear about the **target readers**.
3. Use **readable English**.
4. Use an **appropriate structure**.
5. Use **appropriate formatting** (required).
6. **Get feedback** on the writing.

Effective content = relevant + complete + concise + readable + good figures + well structured.

## 1. Aim of the writing

- The major aim is to earn a good mark — so write to the **marking scheme/rubric**.
- Use the rubric to fix the specific aims of each part (e.g. give an overview of the literature for a general audience; describe research methods, results, and conclusions).
- Every section should describe research methods, results, and conclusions where relevant.

## 2. Target readers

- The dissertation is marked by the **supervisor and a second marker** — but they are **not the only audience**.
- Write so it is accessible to a **broader audience**, usually **peers**.
- Assume the reader **knows the general field (e.g. CS)** but **does not know the specifics of the project**.
- Imagine writing for **yourself before you started the project**.
- Match register to this audience: technical but accessible. The *same* content can be pitched very differently for experts vs. lay readers — pitch it for an informed-but-non-specialist reader and **define jargon** the first time it appears.

## 3. Readable English

### Sentences
- Aim for **shorter sentences**. Avoid too many commas and conjunctions (*and*, *but*, *yet*).
- One sentence = **one unit of information** = one thought or idea.
- If a sentence can be split in two without losing meaning, **split it**.
  - Bad: *"Our model is a modification of Switching Linear Dynamical Systems and we show that it performs better at generation than other models."*
  - Fix: split at *and* into two sentences.

### Words and phrases
- Do **not** use overcomplicated words.
  - Bad: *"In order to fully utilise ion-irradiation to attune the magnetic properties of FeRh it is absolutely essential to have a detailed comprehension of the mechanisms involved."*
- Avoid **stock phrases**, e.g. *"There is a reasonable expectation that…"*.
- Avoid inappropriate jargon. **Define every acronym on first use** and maintain a glossary.

### Cut unnecessary words (edit ruthlessly)
- *"we are in a position to begin a detailed design"* → *"we can begin detailed design"*
- *"This is done by means of inserting an artificial fault"* → *"This is done by inserting an artificial fault"*
- Delete any word that adds nothing to the meaning.

### Active vs. passive
- Active: *"Joe tested the software."* Passive: *"The software was tested by Joe."*
- In scientific writing, passive is generally considered more formal, because the **subject/data matters, not the person**.
- But passive can produce longer, clumsier sentences — **use active only when really needed** to stay clear and concise.

### Personal style / voice
- Avoid an **overly personal** style, but use personal phrasing when it improves readability and avoids contorted sentences. Keep it simple.
- The dissertation is **your** work, not "our" work:
  - Use *"I show…"* rather than *"we show…"*.
  - **Better:** *"The data show…"*.
  - *"We see that by replacing…"* is acceptable — here "we" means you **and** the reader.
  - **Best:** *"Replacing xxx leads to…"*.

### Lists and tables
- Readers get confused by a long list embedded in prose.
- Algorithms, recipes, directions, and other instructions are better written as **lists** or **tables**.
- If you notice you are effectively writing a list, use a **numbered list or bullet points**.

## Required dissertation structure

Produce sections in this order. Use numbered sections.

- **Abstract**
- **Contents**
- **1. Introduction** (including Background — a brief literature review)
- **2. Methodology** — research / experimental methods
- **3. Results and discussion**
- **4. Evaluation and Reflection**
- **5. Conclusion & future work**
- **List of References**

(Front matter such as List of Figures, List of Tables, Declaration, Intellectual Property Statement, and Acknowledgements is also expected per the theses policy.)

### Abstract
- Summarises the **whole dissertation** — it is **not** an introduction. Think **executive summary**.
- Summarise the **results and conclusions** of the work.
- **Self-contained, with no references.**
- **Informative** (state actual results), not merely **descriptive** (saying what the report is about).

### 1. Introduction
- Introduce and overview the dissertation: **What is it about? Why does it matter? What is the research area?**
- **Must include aims and objectives**, and a research question if there is one.
- May describe the structure of the dissertation (e.g. "Chapter 2 covers theory, Chapter 3 the methods").
- **Section introductions:** if a section has sub-sections, the text before the first sub-section must introduce that section.

#### Background (literature review, inside the Introduction)
- **Must include a literature review.**
- State **what is known about the field**.
- Set the work in the **context of what others have done**.
- Keep it **not extensive** — just enough to understand the problem and justify the approach.

### 2. Methodology
- Answer: **how was the project work done?**
- Describe the **tools and techniques** employed or developed.
- Hardware projects: instrumentation and experimental methods.
- Software projects: choice of **programming language, software packages, and methods adopted**.

### 3. Results and discussion
- State **what results were obtained** and **what advances were made**.
- Discuss the **data** (e.g. test results) and what findings can be **deduced** from it.
- Give a detailed discussion of the **validity of conclusions**.
- State **what testing and evaluation** was done.
- Address: **why should the reader believe the results?** Cover **reliability, limitations, and uncertainty** in the methods/outcomes.

### 4. Evaluation and Reflection
- Evaluate the work against its aims/objectives, building on the validity, reliability, and limitations raised in Results and discussion.
- Reflect on what was learned and on the soundness of the approach (the detailed reflection on each objective belongs here and/or in the Conclusion).

### 5. Conclusion & future work
- More important than people think.
- State **what was learned** (positive **or** negative).
- Give the **overall conclusions**.
- **Reflect on progress against each objective** and demonstrate what was learned.
- Discuss **future avenues of investigation**.

### List of References
References must contain, depending on type:
- **Journal articles:** Authors, Title, Journal, Volume number, Page range, Date.
- **Books:** Authors, Title, Edition, Publisher, Date.
- **Conference articles:** Authors, Title, and either Conference Title (Place, Date) or Volume Title, Editors (Publisher, Date).
- Check real examples in journals, theses, and dissertations for acceptable formatting.

## Formatting requirements (required)

Correct presentation is a **requirement**, not optional.

### Section numbering
- Provide enough section numbers for a coherent structure.
- Sections at the **same level** should be **similar in length** (Introduction and Conclusion may be somewhat shorter).
- Use sub-sections (`1.2`) and sub-sub-sections (`1.2.1`), but **avoid sub-sub-sub-sections** (`1.2.1.2`).
- Let the tooling manage numbers and cross-references. In LaTeX use `\label{key}` and `\ref{key}`.

### Figures and tables
- A picture paints a thousand words — good figures are a key part of the work.
- **Always include an informative caption.** Do not forget captions.
- **Always integrate** the figure into the text (refer to it explicitly).
- **Number by section**, e.g. Figure 5.2 is the 2nd figure in Section 5.
- The figure + caption should be **self-contained** — understandable without reading the body text.
- Use `\label{key}` / `\ref{key}` for figure numbers in LaTeX.

### Maths and equations
- Use a distinct font for maths (**italic** or **bold**), especially inline, e.g. "where *α* refers to amplitude".
- Anything longer than a trivial expression (e.g. `x + y = z`) goes on **its own line**.
- **Number every equation.**
- **Punctuate maths like English** — write it the way it is read.
- Use proper maths objects; for substantial maths, use LaTeX.

### References / citations
Two main styles — pick one and apply it consistently:
- **Numerical**, in order of appearance: `[1]` or superscript. E.g. "Hartley showed that… [1]".
- **Author–date (alphabetical):** "Hartley (1957) showed that…" or "It was shown that… (Hartley, 1957)".
- Word and LaTeX generate the reference list automatically. In LaTeX use **BibTeX**, and **natbib** for author–date style.

## Aim and objectives (definition + example)

- **Aim:** a broad statement of the overall intended outcome of the project. **One sentence only.**
- **Objectives:** specific, measurable steps that achieve the aim. **Typically 3–5 items.**

Worked example:

- **Aim:** To develop and evaluate a machine learning model for accurately predicting customer churn in a subscription-based service.
- **Objectives:**
  1. Conduct a literature review on existing churn-prediction models and key influencing factors.
  2. Preprocess and analyse a real-world customer dataset to identify relevant features.
  3. Design and implement multiple ML models (e.g. logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost) for churn prediction.
  4. Evaluate model performance using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.
  5. Interpret results and recommend customer-retention strategies.

## Feedback workflow

- Get feedback on the **overall structure early**.
- Then get feedback on **individual sections** — leave enough time for this.
- **Only submit text for feedback once it follows these rules and has been spell-checked** — otherwise the supervisor is just an expensive spell checker.
- Consider **peer review** among classmates.

## Pre-submission checklist

- [ ] Within ~8,000 words; single PDF; all required pages present.
- [ ] Structure follows the required section order; sections numbered; same-level sections balanced in length; no `1.2.1.2`-depth headings.
- [ ] Abstract is an informative executive summary with results and **no references**.
- [ ] Introduction states aim, objectives, and (if any) research question, and includes a brief literature review setting context.
- [ ] Methodology explains *how* the work was done (tools, languages, techniques).
- [ ] Results and discussion cover findings, validity, reliability, limitations, and uncertainty.
- [ ] Evaluation/Reflection and Conclusion reflect on each objective, state what was learned, and propose future work.
- [ ] Every figure/table has an informative caption, is numbered by section, and is referred to in the text.
- [ ] Every equation is numbered; maths uses a distinct font and is punctuated.
- [ ] One consistent reference style; every reference has all required fields.
- [ ] Sentences are short and single-idea; complicated words, stock phrases, and filler removed; acronyms defined on first use.
- [ ] Active/passive used appropriately; voice is "I"/"the data show…", not "we" for sole-author claims.
- [ ] Spell-checked and proofread; complies with Presentation of Theses Policy
