\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{ch:evaluation}

This chapter evaluates the work against the objectives set out in \cref{sec:aims},
building on the validity discussion in \cref{sec:res-validity}: each objective is
judged against the evidence, the engineering challenges that shaped the result are
stated, and the limitations of what was measured are drawn together. The
\emph{critical reflection} --- what was achieved, what fell short, and what would be
done differently --- is deliberately held back to \cref{sec:reflection}, so that this
chapter reports and judges the work while the next steps back from it.

\section{Evaluation against the objectives}
\label{sec:eval-objectives}

Each objective from \cref{sec:aims} is judged in turn against the evidence in
\cref{ch:results}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Literature and scheme selection --- met.} The adaptor signature was
        selected as a representative exotic scheme with high blockchain value, and
        LAS \cite{esgin2020post} as the design with the clearest path from a standardised
        lattice signature, following the survey's framing of the gap
        \cite{buser2023survey} and the supervisor's steer toward the simplest viable
        construction.
  \item \emph{Additive implementation --- met.} The lattice core is reused
        byte-for-byte (\cref{tab:reuse}); zero upstream functions were modified, which
        the two-branch diff makes auditable. The adaptor layer, wire encoding, and
        application are new code.
  \item \emph{Validation --- met.} Functional, serialization, tamper, and
        known-answer tests all pass (\cref{sec:res-correctness}), establishing the
        adaptor contract, wire-encoding integrity, and reproducibility. Beyond the
        single-codebase tests, an independent Rust implementation reproduces the wire
        format and the pinned known-answer digest byte-for-byte
        (\cref{sec:res-rust}), the strongest correctness evidence available short of
        a formal proof.
  \item \emph{Fair benchmarking --- met.} The primary comparison holds algorithm,
        parameters, and primitives fixed between LAS and its own simplified base, so the
        primary comparison isolates the adaptor overhead --- the base-signature path
        against the LAS adaptor path, every operation timed independently. The adaptor
        overhead is reported per operation as the headline (\cref{tab:overhead-l3}),
        at \emph{both} declared measurement boundaries --- the core tier and the
        packed tier --- so no reported speed reflects an undocumented
        benchmarking scope (\cref{sec:benchmethod}); the absolute timings and a
        parameter-sensitivity check across four settings are shown
        as supporting context (\cref{fig:timing,fig:overhead}, exact
        mean\,$\pm$\,standard-deviation figures in \cref{tab:timing}); computation and
        communication are separated and broken down by component. The optimised CRYSTALS-Dilithium
        reference is included only as context (not algorithm-matched), and a classical
        ECDSA adaptor as a functionality-matched ``price of post-quantum'' baseline,
        each with the standard five metrics. The measurements themselves are defended
        two ways: every run gates on a directly counted rejection rate matching its
        closed-form prediction, and the Rust port re-measures the same operations
        under a different compiler and an independent statistical harness, reproducing
        every relative conclusion (\cref{tab:rust}).
  \item \emph{Atomic-swap demonstration --- met at the simulated-ledger level.} An
        end-to-end two-party, two-chain swap settles both legs and asserts
        unspendability before adaptation (\cref{sec:res-app}). A full deployment on a
        live testnet was not in scope; instead, on a local EVM both an on-chain settlement
        floor and a \emph{complete}, validated native LAS verification are measured
        (\cref{sec:res-app}; \cref{tab:onchain}), the latter at approximately 56.5 million
        gas --- above the
        EIP-7825 per-transaction gas cap, and thus concrete evidence for why deployment
        needs a precompile or a succinct proof. An optimistic (Naysayer) scheme was
        additionally implemented and measured: it makes honest settlement cheap, but its
        worst-case fraud proof still exceeds the per-transaction cap in the evaluated
        baseline (\cref{sec:res-app}), so it does not, on its own, resolve deployability.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Challenges encountered during the modification}
\label{sec:eval-challenges}

Turning the existing simplified-Dilithium base into LAS was conceptually a small
change --- four added operations and one extra term in a hash --- but four
challenges consumed most of the engineering effort, and a fifth arose later in taking
the verifier on-chain. Stating them explicitly is part of explaining how the results
were produced.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The rejection-bound budget.} The single most delicate line of the
        scheme is PreSign's bound $\gamma-\kappa-1$ (\cref{eq:signbound}). Setting it
        one looser, at the base scheme's $\gamma-\kappa$, produces an implementation
        that passes every local test --- pre-signatures pre-verify, extraction works
        --- yet whose \emph{adapted} signatures can exceed the basic verifier's bound
        and fail. The failure is silent, probabilistic, and appears two operations
        away from its cause, so it had to be prevented by reasoning through the norm
        budget ($\inftynorm{y}\le 1$, hence
        $\inftynorm{\hat z + y}\le\gamma-\kappa$) rather than discovered by testing.
  \item \textbf{The reference's optimisations fight the adaptor identity.} The
        adaptor mechanism rests on the exact identity $A\vecz - c\,t = w + Y$. The
        reference implementation deliberately breaks exactness for compactness ---
        Power2Round, the hint vector, and high/low-bit decomposition all discard
        low-order information. Understanding \emph{which} optimisations had to be
        disabled, and why the identity fails with each of them enabled, was a
        prerequisite to a working PreVerify at all; it is also why the simplified
        scheme is a design requirement rather than a convenience
        (\cref{sec:impl}).
  \item \textbf{Living inside another scheme's namespace.} The reused reference
        globally defines single-letter parameter macros --- its \texttt{N} is the ring
        degree and its \texttt{K} the module rank --- while LAS needs its own,
        different dimensions with the same natural names. An innocently named LAS
        constant silently inherits the wrong value through the preprocessor instead
        of failing to compile. This forced a strict naming discipline (prefixed LAS
        parameters, a documented C--Rust--paper name map) and explains some
        otherwise-surprising names in the code.
  \item \textbf{Benchmarking a variable-time scheme fairly.} Rejection sampling makes
        sign-class timings heavy-tailed, and two early artefacts showed how easily
        this misleads: an acceptance rate first estimated from a timing ratio was
        biased (it implied $\approx\!23\%$ where direct counting gives
        \rejAccBase\%), and the two languages' base signing paths initially used
        different norm-check strategies (full-scan versus early-exit) \emph{inside}
        the timed loop --- functionally identical, KAT-invisible, but a work-profile
        asymmetry that biased the primary overhead comparison. Both lessons are now
        institutionalised in the methodology: attempts are counted directly and
        gated against theory (\cref{sec:benchmethod}), the drivers mirror each other
        line-for-line, and every measurement boundary is declared.
  \item \textbf{Verifying on-chain, and why the reference is the only arbiter.} Porting
        \texttt{base\_verify} to Solidity to \emph{measure} the on-chain gas
        (\cref{sec:res-app}) surfaced a trap sharper than any arithmetic bug. The public
        matrix $A'$ is stored by the reference \emph{already in the NTT domain} --- its
        uniform sampler emits the transformed matrix directly --- and a first verifier
        transformed it a second time. The error was \emph{self-consistent}: the recomputed
        commitment $w'$ and the recomputed challenge hash agreed \emph{with each other}, so
        every primitive checked in isolation passed, yet the whole disagreed with the C
        reference, and was found only by an end-to-end check that recomputes the challenge
        digest against the reference's. The lesson shaped the port's whole testing method:
        for a cryptographic re-implementation, a shared cross-implementation golden value is
        the only reliable arbiter and internal self-consistency is \emph{not} correctness,
        which is why the verifier was validated in stages against vectors exported from the
        C code --- each primitive (SHAKE256, the NTT, SampleInBall, the response decode),
        then the assembled verifier, must match --- rather than merely made to run. A
        second, related mismatch --- the reference NTT is Montgomery-domain whereas the
        reused third-party transform is normal-domain, so the two cannot share transformed
        data --- had to be resolved by recovering the normal-domain matrix before reuse.
        One caveat belongs with the figure: those reused primitives come from an
        experimental, self-described non-production library, so the reported gas is a
        faithful cost of \emph{a} numerically-correct verifier, not an endorsement of that
        library for deployment.
\end{enumerate}

A sixth, cross-cutting difficulty --- reproducing the C scheme byte-for-byte in Rust,
down to sampler subtleties such as how each language's uniform sampler discards
rejected blocks --- is discussed with the cross-validation results
(\cref{sec:res-rust}); the pinned known-answer digest was the tool that turned that
difficulty into a mechanical, checkable process.

\section{Reflection in brief}
\label{sec:eval-reflection}

One observation belongs with the results themselves, because it governs how they
should be read. The measurements above are only as trustworthy as the boundary they
were taken at, and two early artefacts in this project produced plausible-looking but
wrong figures --- an acceptance rate inferred from a timing ratio, and two language
drivers doing subtly different work inside the timed loop
(\cref{sec:eval-challenges}). Both were caught by cross-checking against an
independent source rather than by inspection, which is why every number reported here
is tied to a declared measurement tier and gated against a closed-form prediction.
The fuller reflection --- on the strategy, on what fell short, and on what a second
attempt would change --- is developed in \cref{sec:reflection}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:eval-limitations}

The limitations follow from the validity discussion in \cref{sec:res-validity} and
are restated compactly here. There is no formal security analysis of the chosen
parameter set, by design. The modulus is $q\approx 2^{23}$ rather than the
$2^{24}$ specified in \cite{esgin2020post}. The witness is extracted exactly,
sidestepping the relaxed,
noisy-witness setting of \cite{esgin2020post} and the witness growth it implies for long channels. And the
simplified, hint-free scheme produces larger keys and signatures than optimised
Dilithium. Each of these is a deliberate scope choice that trades cryptographic
optimisation for a transparent, testable artefact, and each maps directly to an item
of future work in \cref{sec:future}.
