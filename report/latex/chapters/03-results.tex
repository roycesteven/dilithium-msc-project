\chapter{Results and discussion}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports what was measured and why the results should be believed:
correctness, computation cost, communication cost, and the application results.

\section{Correctness and robustness}
\label{sec:res-correctness}

No performance claim means anything until the implementation is shown \emph{correct},
and an adaptor signature is not correct merely because it signs and verifies --- it must
satisfy a specific contract. \Cref{tab:contract} lists every clause; all
pass. Clauses~1--4 are the adaptor contract itself: a pre-signature is
verifiable yet unspendable (the tripwire), becomes an ordinary signature once the witness
is added, and completing it reveals that witness --- the mechanism that makes an atomic
swap atomic. Clauses~5--6 are robustness, clause~7 reproducibility.

\section{Computation cost}
\label{sec:res-compute}

The primary computation result is the cost of the \emph{adaptor layer}. The two paths
share algorithm, parameters and primitives, so any difference is purely the price of
adaptor functionality (\cref{sec:benchmethod}). \Cref{tab:overhead-l3} reports it at
\emph{both} boundaries and \cref{fig:timing} visualises the core tier: every adaptor
operation stays within single digits of its counterpart, Extract is the cheapest of all,
and every operation completes in well under two milliseconds, signing and pre-signing
dominating because of rejection sampling.

At the core boundary the adaptor layer is almost free, and the reasons are structural.
PreSign ($+\ovPreSign\%$ over Sign) runs the same reject loop, adding only $Y$ to the
commitment and a tighter bound; PreVerify ($+\ovPreVerify\%$) recomputes the same
commitment shape with the extra $+Y$; Adapt ($+\ovAdapt\%$) runs a PreVerify then adds
the witness vector; Extract merely subtracts $s=z-\hat z$ and checks $A\,s=Y$. None changes
the asymptotic work. This is the headline of the standalone-signature evaluation:
\emph{at the core tier} adaptor functionality costs single-digit percentages at every
parameter set, and the two operations unique to an adaptor signature cost about a
verification or less each (\cref{fig:overhead}).

At the packed boundary the premium grows to $+\packedOvPreSign\%$,
$+\packedOvPreVerify\%$ and $+\packedOvAdapt\%$ --- an order of magnitude higher, and the
gap is the statement's decode rather than adaptor arithmetic. The object dominating
\emph{communication} cost thus reappears as the dominant \emph{computation} cost once
bytes are included, so a deployment verifying from bytes should budget for the packed row
--- and reported lattice timings are notoriously sensitive to this boundary
\cite{riou2026apples}.

Signing and pre-signing are dearer than verification because of rejection sampling,
intrinsic to the Fiat--Shamir-with-aborts family rather than a defect here.
\Cref{eq:rejacc} predicts 2.719 attempts per Sign and 2.775 per PreSign, the latter
restarting about $2\%$ more often because its bound is tighter by one; counted directly
rather than estimated from a timing ratio, the run of record observed \rejAttBase{} and
\rejAttLas{} (\cref{tab:rejstats}). At 2500 timed calls the gate band
($\pm\!\sim\!8\%$) is wider than that $2\%$ separation, so the core-tier PreSign overhead
($+\ovPreSign\%$) should be read as of the same order as the prediction rather than as a
precise constant; only the $\approx\!10^5$-call Criterion harness resolves it.

\section{Cross-implementation check: C implementation versus Rust port}
\label{sec:res-rust}

The scheme exists twice (\cref{sec:rustport}), and the two implementations agree at every
level at which they can be compared. At the byte level agreement is exact: the same packed
sizes (public key \pkBytesTarget{}~B, signature \sigBytesTarget{}~B, checked
programmatically on every Rust run) and the same pinned known-answer digest
(\texttt{\katDigestHead\ldots\katDigestTail}). Since that digest binds the packed key
pair, signature, pre-signature and adapted signature, a divergence escapes it only by
leaving every one of those bytes unchanged.

Timing agreement is looser, the builds going through different compilers, but close:
every operation agrees within about \rustCMaxDev\% (largest gap \rustCMaxDevOp). More
importantly the central conclusion reproduces in both languages. At the core tier the Rust
port measures $+\rustOvPreSign\%$, $\rustOvPreVerify\%$ and $+\rustOvAdapt\%$ --- all

the measurement floor rather than a real speed-up, the genuine overhead at this scale

\section{Communication cost and component breakdown}
\label{sec:res-comm}

The computation results show the adaptor layer is cheap at the core boundary; the size
results show where the real cost lies. \Cref{tab:complete-l3} decomposes every LAS object
into bytes at the target setting, explaining \emph{which} component drives the size.
Sizes are fixed by the encoding, so they are exact and carry no dispersion.

Two points follow. First, \textbf{the response $z$ is essentially the entire signature}
--- \zPctTarget\% of it at every parameter set --- since the challenge travels only as its
short digest, so any optimisation of LAS signatures is an optimisation of $z$. Second,
\textbf{the adaptor layer additionally transmits a public-key-sized statement $Y$ and a
signature-sized pre-signature} --- the only objects a basic signature does not send; the
swap also carries $\pi$.

\noindent\textbf{The sharp conclusion: the cost of LAS is not adaptor computation but
communication --- specifically $z$ and $Y$.} The adaptor operations add at most
$+\ovMaxAll\%$ of compute, while the objects on the wire are kilobytes. That matters more
in a blockchain setting than ``the signature is \sigBytesTarget{} bytes'': bytes reaching
a ledger are stored and replicated by every node, while the statement and pre-signature,
though off-chain, still cost both parties bandwidth. The response is stored at full width,
neither range-reduced nor entropy-coded --- the clearest opportunity for reducing that
footprint (\cref{sec:future}).

\subsection{The price of post-quantum: classical adaptor baseline}
\label{sec:res-classical}

The second baseline is a classical secp256k1 ECDSA adaptor signature
\cite{secp256k1zkp}: the natural functional counterpart of LAS, driving the same
scriptless swaps but resting on discrete-log rather than lattice hardness. It measures
the practical cost of moving from a classical adaptor signature to a post-quantum one.
LAS is instantiated at \emph{Simplified Dilithium-II} and only there: secp256k1 offers
roughly 128-bit classical security, so those dimensions are the most like-for-like
engineering match, while -III or -V would pit the classical scheme against a deliberately
stronger configuration. The boundaries differ between the two libraries, so
\cref{tab:classical} matches them per operation and the comparison is read
conservatively --- the conclusions rest on order-of-magnitude gaps, not percent-level
differences.

Two findings stand out. First, \textbf{the post-quantum price is paid almost entirely in
size}: LAS's signature and public key are roughly $\clRatioSig\times$ and
$\clRatioPk\times$ larger than the classical adaptor's, while every LAS operation stays
well under a millisecond. Even the largest per-operation factor (\clRatioTimeMaxOp,
$\clRatioTimeMax\times$) repays decomposition, because it compares two different
\emph{quantities of work} rather than two speeds for the same work: Algorithm~2 of
\cite{esgin2020post} obliges \textsc{Adapt} to run \textsc{PreVerify} before returning, so
\adaptPreVerifyPct\% of LAS's \lasAdaptPackedUs\,$\mu$s is a full lattice verification and
the rest is largely codec, whereas \texttt{secp256k1\_ecdsa\_adaptor\_decrypt} merely
inverts one scalar and multiplies, leaving verification to a separate exported call
\cite{secp256k1zkp}. Charging the classical side the verification LAS cannot skip puts the
two at \clAdaptMatchedUs\,$\mu$s against \lasAdaptPackedUs\,$\mu$s ---
$\clRatioAdaptMatched\times$, not $\clRatioTimeMax\times$: a property of the two
\emph{interfaces}, not of lattice arithmetic. Second, \textbf{the two schemes pay their
adaptor overhead in opposite ways}. The classical adaptor must attach a
discrete-logarithm-equality proof \cite{fournier2019adaptor}, charging its own PreSign
and PreVerify $\clOvPreSignX\times$ and $\clOvPreVerifyX\times$ their base operations,
whereas LAS folds the statement into a hash it already computes. Relative to each
scheme's own base operations, the lattice adaptor layer adds less computational
overhead.

\section{Testing the simplification: the adaptor on unmodified ML-DSA}
\label{sec:res-mldsa}

\Cref{sec:impl} disabled Dilithium's hint vector, Power2Round and high/low-bit
decomposition, reasoning that the adaptor needs the exact identity $A\vecz-c\,t=w+Y$.
That was an \emph{assertion}, so it was tested: the four adaptor operations were built a
third time on NIST ML-DSA \emph{as FIPS~204 specifies it} \cite{1274601}, all three
optimisations enabled. Three findings follow; the second overturns the assertion.

\textbf{The naive port fails outright.} Adding the statement to the challenge hash and
changing nothing else --- treating the hint machinery as a black box --- yields
pre-signatures that do not pre-verify at all (\mldsaNaiveP{}/\mldsaIters{}), because the
transmitted hint reconstructs $\mathrm{HighBits}(w)$ while the challenge committed to
$\mathrm{HighBits}(w+Y)$. Some modification is therefore genuinely unavoidable.

\textbf{But the verifier is not what must change.} Once the \emph{whole} commitment path
moves onto $w+Y$ --- the committed high bits, the low-bits rejection test \emph{and}
\textsc{MakeHint} --- and \textsc{PreSign} tightens its bound by the witness norm, the
construction satisfies the full adaptor contract (\mldsaContract{} items, including four
tamper rejections and byte-identical determinism), and, decisively, \textbf{the unmodified
FIPS~204 verifier accepts the adapted signature} (\mldsaRepairedP{}/\mldsaIters{}). So
\textsc{PreSign} and \textsc{PreVerify} are necessarily new algorithms, while
\textsc{Verify} is not: a post-quantum adaptor swap could settle against a standard ML-DSA
verifier. A matched no-statement baseline reproduces FIPS~204's own published repetition
rates, which is what makes these rows attributable to the adaptor rather than to a porting
error.

\textbf{The size gain is real but bounded, and it relocates the bottleneck.} Measured
against the scheme of record in one process, the adaptor layer stays single-digit on both
constructions ($+\mldsaOvPreSign\%$ against $+\mldsaOvPreSignSimp\%$ for \textsc{PreSign}
per attempt), and per-signature cost is close to equal for opposite reasons: ML-DSA
restarts about twice as often (\mldsaAttempts{} against \mldsaAttemptsSimp{}) but each
attempt is roughly half the price. What ML-DSA buys is \emph{bytes} --- the signature
falls to \mldsaSigBytes{}\,B from \mldsaSigBytesSimp{}\,B --- but the statement $Y$ does
not shrink at all (byte-identical at \mldsaStmtBytes\,B), because Power2Round compresses
a public key while $Y$ enters the verification identity \emph{before} any rounding. The
swap payload therefore improves only to \mldsaPayloadRatio$\times$, and $Y$ becomes larger
than the signature it accompanies. \textbf{Compressing the signature moves the bottleneck
to the statement rather than removing it}, redirecting the size question of
\cref{sec:res-comm} and the future work of \cref{sec:future}.

This is a functional demonstration, not a security argument: whether committing to
$\mathrm{HighBits}(w+Y)$ preserves ML-DSA's unforgeability is not analysed, and is
exactly the kind of claim \cref{sec:eval-limitations} declares out of scope.

\section{Application demonstration}
\label{sec:res-app}


The implementation drives an end-to-end post-quantum atomic swap following the swap
protocol of \cite{esgin2020post}, including its proof of knowledge $\pi$
(\cref{fig:swapidea,fig:swapflow}). Two results matter. First, the protocol runs and
every step is
asserted, including the counterfactuals that a pre-adaptation signature fails basic
verification and that $\pi$ is rejected against any other statement. Second, $\pi$ is what
makes the second party's final step \emph{guaranteed} rather than merely expected: by the
Module-SIS uniqueness argument of \cite{esgin2020post} the extracted witness equals the
proven ternary one, so the adapted signature is certain to clear the verification bound
--- without $\pi$ a malicious counterparty could claim and leave an unadaptable
pre-signature behind. The proof measures \cmpProofTo\,B on the wire at a knowledge error
below $2^{-\piKnowledgeError}$ and is exchanged off-chain only.

As a supporting check on deployability, native on-chain LAS verification was
\emph{implemented} and measured on a real Solidity stack: one claim transaction running
full verification and releasing one leg's escrow costs $\approx$\gasLasVerifiedM{} million
gas (\cref{tab:onchain}; \cref{fig:onchain}), $\approx$\gasRatioVerified{}$\times$ the
classical claim and over the EIP-7825 cap. The application itself is built on the UTXO
ledger \cite{esgin2020post} assumes (\cref{sec:stage2method}); this measures what a
gas-metered venue charges for the same verification.

That figure measures one \emph{implementation}, not the scheme. A restructured verifier
--- same parameters, bounds and challenge preimage, differing in how the computation is
expressed --- was mined by a real client, running full LAS verification and releasing the
escrow at \gasOptReceipt{} gas: \gasOptCapPct\% of the EIP-7825 cap, which bounds the
transaction gas limit and so covers calldata as well as execution. Its equivalence is
conditional on a registration invariant, and both are set out in \cref{tab:onchain}.
On-chain verification therefore fits in one transaction at \emph{Simplified
Dilithium-III} for the 32-byte signed message and signature instance measured, with a
thin margin. Verification alone at \emph{Simplified Dilithium-II}, charged as one
transaction, is \gasDTwoCapPct\% of the cap; at \emph{Simplified Dilithium-V} one
transaction is exceeded, derived rather than measured (\cref{app:methoddetail}). The
contrast with the application's own venue remains: the UTXO constraint
is a transaction \emph{weight} limit one settlement sits well inside
(\cref{sec:res-txstruct}).

\section{The swap on a UTXO chain: three configurations compared}
\label{sec:res-stage2}

The demonstration above establishes that the protocol runs; this section measures what
it \emph{costs}, over the UTXO ledger and under the design of \cref{sec:stage2method}:
three configurations (\cref{tab:configs}), of which only the $2\to3$ step is controlled.
Timings are the mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation over \stageTwoRuns{} complete swaps
at the \emph{packed} tier, so they are not comparable with \cref{sec:res-compute}. Both
LAS configurations passed the rejection gate (\cfgTwoRejMeasured{} attempts per
\textsc{PreSign} against the closed-form \cfgTwoRejExpected{}).


\paragraph{The proof, not the signature, is the cost of a post-quantum swap.}
The role-A proof consumes \cfgTwoProofPct{}\% of end-to-end time in configuration~2 and
\cfgThreeProofPct{}\% in configuration~3. Strip it out and the entire post-quantum
signature workload --- two key generations, a statement, two pre-signatures, a
pre-verification, two adaptations, an extraction and two settlements --- costs
\cfgTwoNonProofUs{}\,$\mu$s, about \stageTwoCryptoRatio{}$\times$ the classical swap's
\cfgOneNonProofUs{}\,$\mu$s. A factor of five on a millisecond-scale workload is
unremarkable; a proof of hundreds of milliseconds decides whether the protocol feels
instantaneous. Even at the packed tier the signature workload is not the bottleneck ---
the post-quantum penalty is not where intuition puts it.

\paragraph{The controlled comparison inverts the expected trade-off.} Replacing Groth16
with LaZer --- same signature, same $\mathbf{A}$, same inputs --- makes the swap
\emph{faster} by \cmpTimePctAbs{}\% while making it \emph{larger} by
\cmpBytesPctAbs{}\%: roughly twice as quick to produce a proof, yet emitting one about
\stageTwoProofSizeRatio{}$\times$ bigger. Groth16 is \emph{succinct} --- three group
elements whatever the circuit --- but producing that proof means a multi-scalar
multiplication over the whole constraint system, whereas LaZer's proof grows with the
statement yet proves a lattice relation with lattice machinery, avoiding the encoding of
a 23-bit modulus into a 254-bit pairing-friendly field. Verification runs the other way
(\cfgTwoProofVerifyMean{}\,$\mu$s against \cfgThreeProofVerifyMean{}), succinctness
paying off exactly where it is designed to.

\paragraph{Configuration~2's trade is worse than it looks.} Pairing security is broken
by Shor, so its proof is precisely the component a post-quantum adversary attacks: a
post-quantum signature does not protect a swap whose fairness rests on a classical proof.
Groth16 also needs a \emph{trusted, per-circuit} setup (\cfgTwoSetupTime{}) whose secret
must be destroyed for soundness, whereas LaZer's parameters are transparent.
Configuration~3 is thus not merely the post-quantum option: it is faster end-to-end,
needs no trusted party and removes a quantum vulnerability, paying only in bytes.

\label{sec:res-txstruct}

\Cref{tab:btctx} answers by projecting the \emph{measured} object sizes onto the

\paragraph{The witness discount absorbs more than half of the post-quantum penalty.}
This rests on a transaction structure Bitcoin did not originally have. Before SegWit
\cite{bip141,bip144} a signature travelled in the input script, where every byte was billed
alike; the segregated witness bills witness bytes at one weight unit against four, and
Taproot \cite{bip341} supplies the output form used here. In raw bytes the post-quantum
settlement is \btcRawRatio$\times$ the classical one; in virtual size --- what fees are
charged on --- only \btcVsizeRatio$\times$, a post-quantum signature being \emph{entirely}
witness data. That decision, taken long before anyone considered lattice signatures on
Bitcoin, removes \btcDiscountSaving\,\% of the size penalty; on the original structure the
same settlement would pay the undiscounted rate throughout. It is a concrete argument for
UTXO chains as the venue, with no counterpart on the EVM, where calldata carries no
comparable discount and native verification cost \gasLasVerifiedM{}\,M gas
(\cref{tab:onchain}).

\paragraph{It fits the weight limits, not the element-size or verification rules.}
A settlement weighs \btcThreeWeight{}\,WU against the \btcMaxStdWeight{}\,WU standardness
limit --- \btcThreePctStd\,\% of it --- and the \btcMaxBlockWeight{}\,WU block limit
admits an upper bound of \btcThreePerBlock{} settlements per block against
\btcOnePerBlock{} classical ones, ignoring competing transactions. A UTXO chain does
impose a limit, then, but a weight limit rather than a gas limit, and one swap sits well
inside it.

Two obstacles survive, neither caused by the adaptor layer. No deployed consensus rule
verifies a lattice signature: Script offers \texttt{OP\_CHECKSIG} over ECDSA or BIP-340
Schnorr and nothing else, so configurations 2 and 3 could not settle on Bitcoin as it
stands (\cref{fig:evmtx}); \cite{esgin2020post} assumes only the venue, a UTXO chain ``where the signature
algorithm is replaced with a lattice-based signature scheme'', and this study takes no
position on which consensus route would deliver it. Separately, the objects collide with
limits that exist regardless: the signature is \btcSigOverElement$\times$ over the 520\,B
consensus stack-item limit and \btcSigOverStdElement$\times$ over the 80\,B standardness
limit \cite{bitcoincorepolicy}. Both obstacles apply equally to an ordinary post-quantum
payment: they are properties of lattice signature sizes, not of adaptor signatures.

\paragraph{Both obstacles were put to a real client.} A stock Bitcoin Core node carries the
lattice objects --- refused by default relay policy, yet consensus-valid and mined --- but
verifies nothing: an ordinary Schnorr signature authorises it. Adding one consensus
rule, an \texttt{OP\_SUCCESS} opcode \cite{bip342} over the byte interface of
\cref{sec:serialize}, gives a node settling a two-leg swap with no elliptic-curve
signature, \btcSwapControls{} mutations tried being refused by it and accepted by an
unmodified node --- the control attributing the refusals to the new rule. The blocker is a consensus rule, not
engineering. Its cost was measured: one verification runs
\btcLasVerifyUs\,$\mu$s against the curve baseline, a BIP340 Schnorr
check's \btcSchnorrVerifyUs\,$\mu$s --- \btcLasRatioSchnorr$\times$ --- and a signature that
fails late costs about as much as one that verifies. The valid-path latency corresponds to a
derived serial rate of \btcLasSerialRateK{}k LAS verifications/s on this machine, not
whole-node or network throughput. A patched node is not Bitcoin, so that
conclusion stands; the exercise is functional, with no security argument (\cref{app:btcnode}).

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:res-validity}

Several factors qualify these results. All timings are machine-dependent, mitigated by
reporting standard deviations, fixing one machine and compiler, emphasising ratios over
absolutes, and by the cross-implementation check (\cref{sec:res-rust}). The parameter
set's concrete security level is not formally established, security proofs being out of
scope, so every cross-scheme claim is qualified by \cref{tab:params}; the modulus is
FIPS~204's $q\approx 2^{23}$ rather than the $2^{24}$ of \cite{esgin2020post}, which
leaves correctness unaffected ($q>2\gamma$) but changes the security margin. The extracted
witness is exact here, whereas the relaxed setting of \cite{esgin2020post} allows witness
noise that can grow across long payment paths.

Two further factors are specific to \cref{sec:res-stage2}. The UTXO ledger is a model
rather than a node (\cref{app:utxomodel}): real transaction objects, real serialised sizes
and the real leak channel atomicity depends on, but no fees or confirmation latency, so
on-chain byte counts are the honest proxy. That limit belongs to the venue rather than the
study: configurations~2 and~3 need a consensus change, built and run in
\cref{app:btcnode}. Finally, the Stage-2 timings come from a single desktop machine
(\stageTwoCpu{}), so the usability discussion is an extrapolation, not a measurement on
constrained hardware.
