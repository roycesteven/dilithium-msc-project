\chapter[Conclusion and future work]{Conclusion, critical reflection, and future work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The aim of \cref{sec:aims} was achieved within the stated prototype scope: the lattice
core is reused unchanged, the adaptor layer, wire encoding and application are new tested
code, the scheme passes functional, serialization, tamper and known-answer tests, an
independent Rust implementation reproduces the pinned digest byte-for-byte, and an
end-to-end two-party, two-chain swap runs.

The central question --- what adaptor functionality costs over a plain signature, and
what post-quantum operation costs over a classical one --- is answered with measured data
at declared boundaries. Adaptor functionality is almost free in computation: at most
$+\ovMaxAll\%$ at the core boundary, rising to
$+\packedOvPreSign$--$\packedOvAdapt\%$ once the byte boundary is included, a rise that is
serialization rather than arithmetic. The cost of LAS is therefore \emph{communication}
--- the signature is \zPctTarget\% response vector, with the statement $Y$ on top --- and
the classical comparison points the same way, the discrete-logarithm-equality proof making
\emph{its} adaptor layer proportionally the more expensive.

One design decision was tested rather than asserted. The simplified base was adopted
because the adaptor appeared to need an exact verification identity; building the same
construction on unmodified ML-DSA showed that belief was half wrong
(\cref{sec:res-mldsa}). \textsc{PreSign} and \textsc{PreVerify} must indeed be new
algorithms --- the naive port fails outright --- but \textsc{Verify} need not be, and a
standard FIPS~204 verifier accepts the adapted signature. That route would halve the
signature at equal computation, yet leave the statement $Y$ untouched, so it relocates
the communication bottleneck rather than removing it.

Taking the protocol to a UTXO ledger --- the venue \cite{esgin2020post} assumes ---
sharpens that conclusion, because at the application level the signature is not the
dominant cost at all: the role-A proof consumes \cfgTwoProofPct\% of a swap under Groth16
and \cfgThreeProofPct\% under LaZer. Changing only the proof system, the
\emph{post-quantum} prover is \cmpTimePctAbs\% faster end-to-end while being
\cmpBytesPctAbs\% larger. The fully post-quantum configuration is thus at once the faster
one, the one with the weaker trust assumption, and the only one Shor does not break,
paying for all three in bytes.

All five objectives were met (\cref{tab:objectives}). The principal qualification is
scope: no concrete security is formally analysed, and both applications run on modelled
ledgers rather than live networks.

\section{Critical reflection}
\label{sec:reflection}

\subsection{What was achieved}
\label{sec:reflection-achieved}

The central strategic choice --- to build \emph{additively} on a standardised reference
rather than reimplement lattice arithmetic --- paid off twice. It made the implementation
tractable, and it produced an unusually strong provenance argument: the security-critical
arithmetic is byte-for-byte the published CRYSTALS-Dilithium reference, with a two-branch
diff that makes the claim auditable rather than assertable. Being language-agnostic, the
strategy also made the second implementation cheap, upgrading the correctness argument
from one tested codebase to two agreeing byte-for-byte. If one result deserves to outlive
this dissertation, it is that pairing a pinned known-answer digest with an independent
reimplementation is a cheap and decisive way to validate a cryptographic port.

Two further things went better than expected. Confronting the fairness problem honestly
--- comparing LAS against a parameter-matched base rather than optimised Dilithium ---
converted an apparent size deficit into a precisely attributed one. And counting
rejection attempts rather than inferring them from a timing ratio corrected a materially
wrong earlier figure, a lesson now institutionalised as a gate every run must pass.

\subsection{What fell short}
\label{sec:reflection-shortfalls}

Three shortfalls are worth stating plainly, over and above the scoped limitations of
\cref{sec:eval-limitations}.

First, and most substantively, \textbf{on-chain settlement was measured but not solved}.
A complete, numerically-correct native Solidity verifier exists and its cost is known
(\cref{tab:onchain}), and a negative result about deployability is still a result. But a
\emph{deployable} post-quantum adaptor swap on a smart-contract chain was not achieved:
native verification sits far above the per-transaction gas cap, and the optimistic variant
only moves the problem into a worst-case fraud proof that also exceeds it. The cause is
structural --- SHAKE256 is not EVM-native --- so local optimisation could not close it.

Second, the applications are \textbf{exploratory demonstrators, not products}. Both
settle over modelled ledgers and assert the properties that matter, but neither runs
against a live network. For the UTXO study this is partly forced: Bitcoin Script cannot
verify a lattice signature, so the post-quantum configurations could not settle on a real
node without a consensus change --- exactly the assumption \cite{esgin2020post} makes.
The reported costs are therefore a feasibility estimate rather than a deployment figure,
and since the omitted overheads add cost, not an upper bound on one.

Third, the concrete security of the chosen parameter set is \textbf{unanalysed by
design}. This was a sanctioned scope decision, not an oversight, but it bounds what
the performance numbers mean: they characterise a faithful, testable artefact at a
stated engineering setting, not a parameter set anyone should deploy.

\subsection{What would be done differently}
\label{sec:reflection-differently}

The clearest change is \textbf{target selection for the application}. Substantial effort
went into taking the verifier on-chain in Solidity before the gas cost was known to be
prohibitive. The framing was wrong as well as the venue: the project asked ``can a
post-quantum signature be verified on chain?'' when what mattered was ``what dominates a
post-quantum swap?'' --- and the answer never touches a chain at all. Beginning on the
UTXO side would have reached that sooner, with the EVM measurement retained for what it
does establish: why the venue was changed.

Second, \textbf{measurement discipline would come first}: both early artefacts --- the
biased acceptance estimate and the asymmetric timed loops --- followed from building the
benchmark before deciding what boundary it measured. Third, in the same spirit, the ML-DSA
experiment should have come \emph{before} the simplified scheme was justified in writing:
a design decision defended by argument alone is one nobody has tested, and testing it took
days, not weeks.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A statement that is small by design.} Building on unmodified ML-DSA
        halves the signature while leaving $Y$ byte-identical (\cref{sec:res-mldsa}), so
        the statement becomes the dominant object. Compressing $Y$ \emph{inside} this
        construction was then tested directly, and fails: truncation is invisible to the
        adaptor's own functions, which hash whatever statement they are handed, and fatal
        at the two boundaries that matter, since ordinary verification recomputes
        $\mathbf{Az}-c\mathbf{t}$ against the true $Y$ and \textsc{Ext} accepts only on the
        exact relation. Deriving $Y$ from its generator seed compresses it completely and
        hands over the witness. What is open is therefore not a better encoding but a
        different hard relation whose statement is small by construction.
  \item \textbf{Reduce the proof.} The role-A proof dominates a swap, and both routes
        proposed here were measured; both fail at this scale. Batching amortises the wrong
        cost --- the Groth16 proof is constant in the batch and was never the bottleneck,
        while the LaZer proof does shrink per swap, but only by making proving and
        verification several times worse. A \emph{succinct} post-quantum system that is
        also zero-knowledge, which \cite{esgin2020post} requires of $\pi$, was run under
        LaBRADOR and loses to the deployed prover on every axis: succinctness is
        asymptotic, and one instance of this relation is far too small to reach it. What
        is open is a construction whose proof is small at \emph{this} scale.
  \item \textbf{Bring on-chain verification within one transaction.} Native verification
        and an optimistic Naysayer variant \cite{kysil2025poqeth} both exceed the
        per-transaction cap in the worst case (\cref{sec:res-app}). The promising routes
        attack the dominant hash term: a dispute avoiding a full SHAKE256 pass or a
        precompile for it, a Merkle opening of the disputed matrix row, or a succinct
        proof of verification.
  \item \textbf{Analyse the security of the ML-DSA variant.} \Cref{sec:res-mldsa}
        establishes that committing to $\mathrm{HighBits}(w+Y)$ is \emph{functionally}
        sound; whether it preserves ML-DSA's unforgeability argument is unexamined, and
        is the prerequisite before that route could be recommended over the simplified
        scheme.
  \item \textbf{Take the UTXO study to a live network.} \emph{Signet} would supply the
        classical configuration with the fees, propagation and latency the model cannot.
        For configurations~2 and~3 no Bitcoin test network helps, and that is the point
        rather than a caveat: the post-quantum swap is blocked on chain support, not on
        engineering --- mirroring the missing-precompile conclusion reached on the EVM.
\end{itemize}
