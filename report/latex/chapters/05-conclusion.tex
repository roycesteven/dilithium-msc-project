\chapter[Conclusion and future work]{Conclusion, critical reflection, and future work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The aim of \cref{sec:aims} was achieved within the stated prototype scope. The lattice
core is reused unchanged; the adaptor layer, wire encoding and application are new,
tested code. The scheme passes functional, serialization, tamper and known-answer tests,
an independent Rust implementation reproduces the wire format and the pinned digest
byte-for-byte, and an end-to-end two-party, two-chain swap runs.

The central question --- what adaptor functionality costs over a plain signature, and
what post-quantum operation costs over a classical one --- is answered with measured
data at declared boundaries. Adaptor functionality is almost free in computation: at most
$+\ovMaxAll\%$ at the core boundary, rising to
$+\packedOvPreSign$--$\packedOvAdapt\%$ once the byte boundary is included, a rise that
is serialization rather than arithmetic. The cost of LAS is therefore
\emph{communication} --- the signature is \zPctTarget\% response vector at every parameter set, with the
statement $Y$ transmitted on top --- and the classical comparison points the same way,
the discrete-logarithm-equality proof making \emph{its} adaptor layer proportionally the
more expensive.

Taking the protocol to a UTXO ledger --- the venue \cite{esgin2020post} actually
assumes --- sharpens that conclusion rather than repeating it, because at the
application level the signature is not the dominant cost at all: the role-A proof of
knowledge consumes \cfgTwoProofPct\% of a swap under Groth16 and \cfgThreeProofPct\%
under LaZer. The controlled comparison then inverts the expected trade-off: changing
only the proof system, the \emph{post-quantum} prover is \cmpTimePctAbs\% faster
end-to-end while being \cmpBytesPctAbs\% larger. The fully post-quantum configuration is
thus at once the faster one, the one with the weaker trust assumption --- LaZer needs no
trusted setup --- and the only one Shor does not break, paying for all three in bytes.

All five objectives were met (\cref{tab:objectives}). The principal qualification is
scope: the parameter set's concrete security is not formally analysed, and both
applications run on modelled ledgers rather than live networks.

\section{Critical reflection}
\label{sec:reflection}

\subsection{What was achieved}
\label{sec:reflection-achieved}

The central strategic choice --- to build \emph{additively} on a standardised reference
rather than reimplement lattice arithmetic --- paid off twice. It made the
implementation tractable in the time available. It also produced an unusually strong
provenance argument: the security-critical arithmetic is byte-for-byte the published
CRYSTALS-Dilithium reference, with a two-branch diff that makes the claim auditable
rather than assertable. Being language-agnostic, the strategy made the second
implementation cheap to build, yet upgraded the correctness argument from one tested
codebase to two that agree byte-for-byte, and exposed every timing conclusion to an
independent harness. If one methodological result deserves to
outlive this dissertation, it is that pairing a pinned known-answer digest with an
independent reimplementation is a cheap and decisive way to validate a cryptographic
port.

Two further things went better than expected. Confronting the fairness problem honestly
--- comparing LAS against a parameter-matched base rather than optimised Dilithium ---
strengthened the conclusions rather than weakening them, converting an apparent size
deficit into a precisely attributed one. And counting rejection attempts rather than
inferring them from a timing ratio corrected a materially wrong earlier figure, a lesson
now institutionalised as a gate every benchmark run must pass.

\subsection{What fell short}
\label{sec:reflection-shortfalls}

Three shortfalls are worth stating plainly, over and above the scoped limitations of
\cref{sec:eval-limitations}.

First, and most substantively, \textbf{on-chain settlement was measured but not
solved}. A complete, numerically-correct native Solidity verifier exists and its cost is
known (\cref{tab:onchain}), and a negative result about deployability is still a result.
But the honest reading is that a \emph{deployable} post-quantum adaptor swap on a
smart-contract chain was not achieved: native verification sits far above the
per-transaction gas cap, and the optimistic variant only moves the problem into a
worst-case fraud proof that also exceeds it. The cause is structural rather than a
failure of tuning, and it decides the future work. The decisive term is the hash,
SHAKE256 not being EVM-native, while the lattice arithmetic compounds the problem
through calldata rather than independently breaching the cap; local optimisation could
close neither.

Second, the applications are \textbf{exploratory demonstrators, not products}. Both
settle over modelled ledgers and assert the properties that matter, but neither runs
against a live network. For the UTXO study this is partly forced: Bitcoin Script cannot
verify a lattice signature, so the post-quantum configurations could not settle on a real
node without a consensus change --- exactly the assumption \cite{esgin2020post} makes
(\cref{app:utxomodel}). The model is faithful to that assumption; what it omits is
network, consensus, fee and confirmation
overhead. The reported costs are therefore an implementation-level feasibility estimate
rather than a deployment figure --- and, since the omitted overheads add cost, certainly
not an upper bound on one.

Third, the concrete security of the chosen parameter set is \textbf{unanalysed by
design}. This was a sanctioned scope decision, not an oversight, but it bounds what
the performance numbers mean: they characterise a faithful, testable artefact at a
stated engineering setting, not a parameter set anyone should deploy.

\subsection{What would be done differently}
\label{sec:reflection-differently}

The clearest change is \textbf{target selection for the application}. Substantial effort
went into taking the verifier on-chain in Solidity before the gas cost was known to be
prohibitive. Adaptor signatures are used in practice for swaps on Bitcoin and other
UTXO-based chains precisely because the heavy work stays off-chain and the chain meters
transactions by \emph{size} rather than execution: Bitcoin still bounds a block, by
weight, but has no per-operation gas metering for a verifier to exhaust. The UTXO study
bears this out --- on that venue the protocol is not merely feasible but unremarkable.
The framing was wrong as well as the venue: the project asked ``can a post-quantum
signature be verified on chain?'' when the measurement that mattered was ``what
dominates a post-quantum swap?'', and the answer proved to be a component that never
touches a chain at all. Beginning on the UTXO side would have reached that question
sooner, with the EVM measurement retained for what it genuinely establishes: the
comparative argument for \emph{why} the venue was changed.

Second, \textbf{measurement discipline would come first, not second}. Both early
artefacts --- the biased acceptance estimate and the asymmetric timed loops --- followed
from building the benchmark before deciding what boundary it measured; declaring the
tiers and installing the theory gate at the outset would have cost a day and saved
considerably more. Third, the \textbf{namespace and provenance discipline} the code now
follows was retrofitted only after the preprocessor had silently supplied a wrong
constant --- on any project modifying a cryptographic reference implementation, that
discipline is cheap at the start and expensive to add later.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reduce the proof, not the signature.} The application study identifies
        the role-A proof as the dominant end-to-end cost by an order of magnitude. Two
        directions are worth measuring: a \emph{succinct} post-quantum proof system,
        cutting the proof from tens of kilobytes toward Groth16's constant size while
        keeping transparency; and amortisation across swaps, since a party proves
        knowledge of a fresh statement each time.
  \item \textbf{Bring on-chain verification within one transaction.} Native
        verification and an optimistic Naysayer variant \cite{kysil2025poqeth} both
        exceed the per-transaction cap in the worst case (\cref{sec:res-app}). The hash
        dispute breaches the cap outright and the matrix work inflates calldata, so the
        promising routes attack both: a hash dispute avoiding a full SHAKE256 pass or a
        precompile for it, a Merkle opening of the single disputed matrix row rather
        than the whole matrix, or a succinct proof of verification.
  \item \textbf{Move bulk data off chain, and ask who then stores it.} Where a design
        genuinely requires a proof to be publicly available --- the on-chain
        verification route above, not the swap, whose $\pi$ is already exchanged
        directly between the two parties --- a content-addressed store such as IPFS
        could hold it while the chain carries only a commitment. The saving is real but
        not free: it introduces a second platform and a bridge to it, and it relocates
        rather than answers the question of who is paid to keep the data available.
  \item \textbf{Closing the size gap, and why it is not routine.} The response $z$ is
        almost the whole signature (\cref{sec:res-comm}) and already occupies the
        minimum \emph{fixed} width its range admits, so shrinking it needs entropy or
        range coding, or a smaller $\gamma$. The reductions that close the gap for plain
        Dilithium are exactly the optimisations this construction had to disable
        (\cref{sec:impl}), so whether an adaptor-compatible analogue exists is a genuine
        open question rather than an engineering task.
  \item \textbf{Parameter migration.} Move to the $q\approx 2^{24}$ of
        \cite{esgin2020post} with a new NTT table and map the parameters to NIST
        security levels, with before-and-after benchmarks so the trade-off is
        explicit.
  \item \textbf{Take the UTXO study to a live network.} For the classical configuration
        \emph{signet} would supply the fees, propagation and confirmation latency the
        model cannot. For configurations~2 and~3 no Bitcoin test network helps, and that
        is the point rather than a caveat: a deployment figure for the post-quantum swap
        is blocked on chain support, not on engineering --- mirroring on the UTXO side
        the missing-precompile conclusion reached on the EVM.
  \item \textbf{A new research question.} Extend toward functional adaptor signatures,
        moving from systems implementation toward a novel construction.
\end{itemize}
