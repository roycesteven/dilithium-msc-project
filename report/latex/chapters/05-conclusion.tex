\chapter{Conclusion, critical reflection, and future work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This dissertation set out to implement and evaluate a post-quantum exotic signature
scheme --- a lattice-based adaptor signature reusing the CRYSTALS-Dilithium
primitives --- and to demonstrate it in a blockchain atomic swap. That aim was
achieved. A complete adaptor signature was built additively on the Dilithium
reference, with the lattice core reused unchanged and the adaptor layer, wire
encoding, and application added as new, tested code. The scheme passes functional,
serialization, tamper, and known-answer tests, is corroborated by an independent
Rust implementation that reproduces the wire format and the pinned known-answer
digest byte-for-byte, and drives an end-to-end two-party,
two-chain atomic swap.

The evaluation answers the project's central question --- what does adaptor
functionality cost over a plain signature, and what does post-quantum operation cost
over a classical one --- with measured data, at explicitly declared measurement
boundaries. At the core boundary, which isolates the adaptor arithmetic, the primary
fair comparison of LAS against its own simplified base shows the adaptor layer is
almost free in computation: PreSign,
PreVerify, and Adapt each add only single-digit overhead (at most $+\ovMaxAll\%$)
over the basic operation they mirror, at every parameter set --- a conclusion both
implementations reproduce independently --- and this is relatively
cheaper than the classical ECDSA
adaptor, whose discrete-logarithm-equality proof dominates its adaptor cost. At the
packed boundary, where every input is decoded from and every output encoded to
wire bytes, the adaptor premium rises to $+\packedOvPreSign$--$\packedOvAdapt\%$ ---
not because the adaptor arithmetic grows, but because each adaptor operation must
additionally decode the public-key-sized statement $Y$. The real
cost is therefore communication, and it is visible on both axes: the signature is
\zPctTwo--\zPctFive\% lattice response vector, an
adaptor swap additionally transmits a public-key-sized statement, and that same
statement dominates the byte-boundary compute cost. The contextual
comparison against the optimised CRYSTALS-Dilithium reference --- not algorithm-matched
--- shows that the remaining size gap is one of packing and production optimisation,
not of security level.

Measured against the objectives, all five were met: the scheme was selected and
justified, implemented by reuse, validated, benchmarked fairly against two baselines
with the standard metrics, and demonstrated in an application. The principal
qualification is scope: the parameter set's concrete security is not formally
analysed, and the application runs on a simulated ledger rather than a live testnet.

\section{Critical reflection}
\label{sec:reflection}

\subsection{What was achieved}
\label{sec:reflection-achieved}

Judged as a whole, the project's central strategic choice --- to build
\emph{additively} on a standardised reference rather than to reimplement lattice
arithmetic --- was the right one, and it paid off twice. It made the implementation
tractable in the time available, and it produced an unusually strong trust argument,
because the security-critical arithmetic is byte-for-byte the audited upstream code
and the two-branch diff makes that claim auditable rather than assertable. The same
property transferred across languages: because the reuse strategy is
language-agnostic, the second (Rust) implementation was comparatively cheap to build,
yet it upgraded the correctness argument from ``one tested codebase'' to ``two
independent codebases that agree byte-for-byte'', and simultaneously exposed every
timing conclusion to an independent statistical harness. If one methodological result
deserves to outlive this dissertation, it is that pairing a pinned known-answer digest
with an independent reimplementation is a cheap and unusually decisive way to validate
a cryptographic port.

Two further things went better than expected. Confronting the fairness problem
honestly --- stating every scheme's parameters and comparing LAS against a
parameter-matched base rather than against optimised Dilithium --- strengthened the
conclusions instead of weakening them, because it converted an apparent size deficit
into a precisely attributed one: a packing gap, not a dimension or security-level gap.
And measuring the rejection rate directly, by counting attempts rather than inferring
it from a timing ratio, corrected a materially wrong earlier figure; that lesson is now
institutionalised as a gate every benchmark run must pass before any timing may be
reported.

\subsection{What fell short}
\label{sec:reflection-shortfalls}

Three shortfalls are worth stating plainly, over and above the scoped limitations of
\cref{sec:eval-limitations}.

First, and most substantively, \textbf{on-chain settlement was measured but not
solved}. A complete, numerically-correct native Solidity verifier now exists and its
cost is known, and that is a genuine contribution --- negative results about
deployability are still results. But the honest reading is that the objective of
demonstrating a deployable post-quantum adaptor swap on a smart-contract chain was
\emph{not} reached: native verification sits far above the per-transaction gas cap,
and the optimistic (Naysayer) variant, while it makes honest settlement cheap, moves
the problem into a worst-case fraud proof that also exceeds the cap. The root cause is
structural rather than incidental --- SHAKE256 is not an EVM-native hash --- which is
precisely why no amount of local optimisation closed it.

Second, the application is an \textbf{exploratory demonstrator, not a product}. It
settles both legs of a two-party swap over a simulated ledger and asserts the
properties that matter, but it does not run against a live testnet, and the protocol
path was deliberately left unoptimised where optimisation would not have changed any
conclusion --- most visibly in serialization, which is reported as its own measurement
tier rather than engineered away. Treating that as a limitation to be declared, rather
than a gap to be quietly papered over, is the right posture for an exploration of
feasibility; it does mean the reported application-level costs should be read as an
upper bound on a tuned implementation rather than as a deployment figure.

Third, the concrete security of the chosen parameter set is \textbf{unanalysed by
design}, and the modulus differs from the one specified in \cite{esgin2020post}. This
was a sanctioned scope decision, not an oversight, but it bounds what the performance
numbers mean: they characterise a faithful, testable artefact at a stated engineering
setting, not a parameter set anyone should deploy.

\subsection{What would be done differently}
\label{sec:reflection-differently}

Given the work again, three decisions would change.

The clearest is \textbf{target selection for the application}. Substantial effort went
into taking the verifier on-chain in Solidity before the gas cost was known to be
prohibitive; the measurements that establish this are among the more useful results
here, but they also show that a smart-contract chain was the harder venue. Adaptor
signatures are used in practice for atomic swaps on Bitcoin and other UTXO-based
chains precisely because the heavy work stays off-chain and there is no per-block
execution budget to exceed. Beginning on a UTXO chain would have produced a working,
defensibly feasible application sooner, with the EVM measurement retained as the
comparative argument for \emph{why} that venue was chosen --- which is the direction
the work now takes (\cref{sec:future}).

Second, \textbf{measurement discipline would come first, not second}. Both early
measurement artefacts --- the biased acceptance estimate and the asymmetric timed
loops --- were the consequence of building the benchmark before deciding exactly what
boundary it measured. Declaring the measurement tiers and installing the theory gate
at the outset would have cost a day and saved considerably more.

Third, the \textbf{namespace and provenance discipline} that the code now follows
(prefixed parameters, a documented C--Rust--paper name map, a strict reused/modified/added
classification) was retrofitted after the preprocessor had already silently supplied a
wrong constant. On any future project that modifies a cryptographic reference
implementation, that discipline is cheap at the start and expensive to add later.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

The results point to several well-motivated directions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tighter packing.} Apply Dilithium-style decomposition and a hint
        vector to the response, and regenerate the public key from a seed, to close
        most of the size gap identified in \cref{sec:res-comm} --- the single largest
        practical improvement available.
  \item \textbf{Parameter migration.} Move to the $q\approx 2^{24}$ of
        \cite{esgin2020post} with a new
        NTT table, and map the parameters to NIST security levels, reporting
        before-and-after benchmarks so the security/cost trade-off is explicit.
  \item \textbf{On-chain verification.} A complete, numerically-correct native Solidity
        LAS verifier is now implemented and measured (\cref{sec:res-app}; approximately
        56.5 million gas, above the EIP-7825 per-transaction cap of $16{,}777{,}216$). A
        poqeth-inspired optimistic \emph{Naysayer} verifier \cite{kysil2025poqeth} was then
        implemented and measured as a candidate remedy: it makes honest settlement cheap
        (an optimistic claim is approximately $1.1$ million gas), but its worst-case fraud
        proof already exceeds the cap on execution gas alone --- a Solidity SHAKE256 hash
        dispute at approximately $29.4$ million gas, and a full-matrix commitment dispute at
        approximately $13.9$ million gas of execution (its approximately $246$-kilobyte
        calldata pushing the transaction total higher still) --- because SHAKE256 is not an
        EVM-native hash. Bringing settlement within a single transaction therefore remains
        open; the most promising routes are a Merkle opening of the single disputed matrix
        row, a hash dispute that avoids a full SHAKE256 pass, a dedicated precompile, or a
        succinct (zero-knowledge) proof of verification --- after which a swap should be
        benchmarked on a live testnet.
  \item \textbf{Retarget the application to a UTXO chain.} The gas results above make
        the case for changing venue rather than continuing to optimise against the
        EVM's execution budget. On Bitcoin and other UTXO-based chains --- which is
        where adaptor signatures are actually used in practice, precisely because the
        adaptor layer is confined to the off-chain protocol and the chain only ever
        validates an ordinary signature --- there is no per-transaction execution cap to
        exceed, so a swap's feasibility cannot be dismissed on cost grounds. The natural
        next experiment is a three-way comparison at equal protocol structure: a
        classical adaptor swap, the same swap with LAS substituted for the classical
        signature, and the same swap with the proof of knowledge additionally moved to a
        post-quantum system --- which isolates the cost of the post-quantum signature
        from that of the post-quantum proof. Because gas does not exist in that setting,
        the metrics become execution time and communication cost, the latter counting
        off-chain messages, where the post-quantum premium actually concentrates. One
        qualification must travel with such a result: Bitcoin Script cannot verify a
        lattice signature, so the settlement signature would remain classical and it is
        the swap's \emph{atomicity}, not the coin's authorisation, that becomes
        post-quantum.
  \item \textbf{A new research question.} Extend toward functional adaptor
        signatures, which would move the project from systems implementation toward a
        novel construction and a publishable contribution.
\end{itemize}
